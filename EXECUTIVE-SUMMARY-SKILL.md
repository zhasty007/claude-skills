# Executive Summary

A consulting skill for compressing analysis into the single page that a senior leader will actually read. Most executives read the first paragraph and the recommendations. Everything else is optional.

## When to use this skill

The user has source material (analysis, report, research, deck content) and needs to produce a short document for a senior audience. The skill applies whether the output is a memo, the executive summary slide of a deck, or a pre-read for a meeting.

## What this skill produces

A one-page executive summary structured as:

1. **The answer**, stated in the first two sentences
2. **Why it matters**, in business terms (revenue, cost, risk, time, competitive position)
3. **What we found**, in 3 to 5 short paragraphs or bullet groups
4. **Recommendations**, ranked, with expected impact and effort
5. **What we need from you** (a decision, an approval, a resource commitment), if applicable

## Process

1. **Confirm the audience.** Ask the user: who specifically will read this, what decision are they making, and what is their existing context. A CFO reads differently than a board. A CEO with full context needs less framing than a steering committee meeting for the first time.

2. **Lead with the answer.** The first sentence states the conclusion. The second sentence states the magnitude or implication. Do not lead with methodology, context, or "we were asked to look at." Senior readers want the punchline first.

3. **Translate into business terms.** Replace process language with outcome language. "We analyzed the procurement spend across 47 categories" becomes "Procurement spend can be reduced by $12M annually." Replace consulting jargon with plain language. If the reader's industry has terms of art, use them; otherwise, do not.

4. **Compress the body ruthlessly.** Maximum 250 words for the body of the summary. Each paragraph or bullet group should make one point and end. If a sentence can be cut without losing meaning, cut it. Adverbs and qualifiers ("significantly," "potentially," "approximately") are the first to go unless they carry meaning.

5. **Rank recommendations.** Order by expected impact divided by effort. For each, state:
   - The action (a verb-led sentence)
   - Expected impact (specific, with a number if possible)
   - Effort required (low, medium, high, with rationale)
   - Owner or owner type

6. **End with the ask.** What does the executive need to do after reading. Approve a budget. Decide between options. Sponsor a workstream. Be explicit.

## Output format

- One page, roughly 400 to 500 words total including all sections
- Bold the recommendations and the headline finding
- Use one chart or table if it makes the point faster than prose; otherwise omit
- No footnotes in the summary itself. Detailed support lives in the appendix.

## Tone

Confident but not promotional. State findings as findings, not as advocacy. If uncertainty is material, name it in one sentence rather than hedging throughout. "The $12M estimate has a +/- 20% range pending validation of supplier data" is more credible than peppering the document with "may," "could," and "potentially."

## Constraints

- Do not exceed 500 words total. The one-page constraint is the point.
- Do not invent numbers. If the source material does not contain a number, do not produce one.
- Do not bury the answer. If the first sentence does not state the conclusion, the draft is wrong.
- Do not lead with the methodology, the team, or the engagement scope. Executives assume competence; they want the answer.

## Example trigger phrases

- "Turn this into a one-pager for the CFO"
- "Exec summary of this analysis"
- "Board memo from this report"
- "TL;DR for leadership"
- "This is too long, condense it for the steering committee"
